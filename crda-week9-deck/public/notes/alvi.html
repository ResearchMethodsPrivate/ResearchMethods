Slide 3 · What we ran

start: "Thanks, Anik."≈ 63 s2 clicks

One line: Same code and settings as the paper’s main table, on four laptops, 15 repeats of every run.

  1. Start

    Thanks, Anik. We wanted our runs as close to the paper’s as possible, so that any difference means something. We used the authors’ code unchanged, and copied the settings from their saved files for the main table. That means 15 repeats of every run. A repeat means we run the whole experiment again, with a different random split of the data into training and test. Doing it 15 times shows how much the result moves. We also used the biggest data subset, and their two models, a neural network and XGBoost, each with and without CRDA.

  2. Click 1

    Then we split the work. I ran Energy Efficiency on my Windows laptop. Anik ran the same again on his Mac, as an independent check. Samim ran Concrete Strength and the safety-test experiment. Atul built the comparison table and the problem log.

  3. Click 2

    In total, eight full runs and 120 experiments, six to 22 minutes each, on ordinary laptops with no graphics card.

  1. Start

Slide 7 · The second catch

start: "Thanks, Samim."≈ 96 s3 clicks

One line: Much of CRDA’s gain is just averaging 10 models. For XGBoost, most or all of it. For the neural network: none on Energy Efficiency, about two thirds on Concrete.

  1. Start

    Thanks, Samim. The second catch is one we tested on both datasets. In the authors’ code, the plain score comes from one model. But the CRDA score is the average of 10 models. Averaging usually lowers error by itself. So we scored the plain model the same way: ten plain models, averaged. I ran it on Energy Efficiency, Anik repeated it on his Mac, and Samim ran Concrete Strength. In every case the single plain error matched our earlier runs exactly.

  2. Click 1

    On Energy Efficiency, for the neural network, averaging ten plain models changes nothing, so CRDA’s gain of about 29 percent is real. Anik’s Mac agrees. For XGBoost, averaging alone lowers the error by 15 percent, out of a gain of 22. On top of averaging, CRDA adds 8 percent on my laptop, and 6 on Anik’s Mac, where that is not clearly more than luck.

  3. Click 2

    Concrete Strength shows it more strongly. For the neural network, averaging alone gives 17 percent out of 24, so CRDA adds 9 on top, which is clear. For XGBoost, averaging alone gives 14 percent, and CRDA’s whole gain is 15. On top of averaging, CRDA adds nothing.

  4. Click 3

    So, like for like, CRDA adds less than the paper’s way of scoring suggests. The paper’s headline numbers mix CRDA’s own gain with the gain from averaging. For XGBoost that is most of it, or all of it. We will say which version we report in the final.

Energy Efficiency, neural network

Averaging alone: +0.1% (nothing). CRDA: −28.7%. CRDA on top of averaging: −28.2% (Mac: −29.7%).

Energy Efficiency, XGBoost

Averaging alone: −15.3%. CRDA: −21.7%. On top of averaging: −7.9% (Mac: −6.1%, not clearly more than luck).

Concrete Strength, neural network (Samim)

Averaging alone: −16.7%. CRDA: −24.2%. On top of averaging: −8.7%, which is clear.

Concrete Strength, XGBoost (Samim)

Averaging alone: −14.4%. CRDA: −14.6%. On top of averaging: 0.0%. Nothing.

Roughly, XGBoost on Energy Efficiency: plain single model 100 → average of 10 plain models 85 → CRDA average of 10 78. Averaging alone gets most of the way. Made-up round numbers that follow the real percentages.

Words in plain English

Repeat (seed)
Running the whole experiment again with a different random split of the data into training and test. We did 15.
Error (MSE)
The average size of the model’s mistakes on data it has not seen. Lower is better.
Plain model
The same model without CRDA.
MLP / XGBoost
Two kinds of prediction model: a small neural network, and a set of decision trees.
10-model average
Train 10 models on slightly different slices of the training data and average their predictions. Averaging usually lowers error by itself.

Likely questions

Why 15 repeats?

It is what the paper used, so our numbers compare directly.

What is a repeat?

We run the whole experiment again on a different random split of the data. It shows how much the answer moves.

Why do you, Anik and the paper differ?

Small differences are normal. From one repeat to the next the gain swings from about −67% to +25%, so a few points between laptops is expected.

How do you know your 10-model test is fair?

I re-computed the single plain error and it matches our earlier saved runs exactly on all 15 repeats, for both models. So the split, the settings and the code path are the same.

So is CRDA useless for XGBoost?

Not useless, but on Concrete Strength it adds nothing on top of averaging, and on Energy Efficiency it adds 6 to 8 percent. The paper’s headline mixes both effects.

Does that hold on other datasets or laptops?

We checked two datasets and two kinds of laptop. The XGBoost pattern is the same everywhere: averaging explains most or all of the gain. The neural network differs by dataset.

Why is the neural network different?

On Energy Efficiency, averaging 10 plain neural networks did not lower the error, so the gain is CRDA’s. On Concrete Strength, averaging alone gives 17 of the 24 percent. We do not know why it depends on the dataset.

Why does Windows differ for plain XGBoost?

We do not know yet. Both Windows laptops give the same number, and the Mac matches the paper.

Don't say

  • That CRDA is useless. Say “it adds less than the paper’s way of scoring suggests”.
  • That it holds for every dataset. We tested two.
  • Why Windows differs. Say “we do not know yet”.
  • Exact digits. Round: 28, 29, 32; 21, 22, 18.
